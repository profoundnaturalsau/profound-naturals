Baltic Amber

Baltic Amber oil is one of the world's most extraordinary aromatics - drawn not from living plant material, but from ancient fossilised tree resin. A gemstone in aromatic form.

What It Is

Unlike a true essential oil, Baltic Amber cannot be steam-distilled - the fossilised resin has hardened over millions of years and will not surrender its aroma to water and gentle heat. Instead it is produced by dry distillation: the amber is crushed and taken to high heat until a thick, aromatic oil is drawn from the resin itself. The result is a warm, smoky, deeply resinous oil - viscous and dark, with an earthy sweetness and a leathery, balsamic character that exists nowhere else in nature.

Ancient Civilisations

Ancient Greeks and Egyptians called it the "sun stone," prizing it for its golden light and therapeutic warmth. It was used in healing remedies for rheumatism, fever and digestive conditions, and burned as sacred incense in temples and burial rites.

Medieval Medicine

During the Middle Ages, amber was used to treat respiratory conditions including asthma. In times of plague, people burned amber resin and carried it as a talisman - believed to purify the air and protect against disease.

The Amber Road

From as early as 8000 BC, Baltic Amber was one of the most valuable traded commodities on Earth - carried along the famous Amber Road connecting the Baltic Sea to the Mediterranean, exchanged weight-for-weight with gold and precious metals.

Traditional Chinese Medicine

Known as Hu Po, Baltic Amber has been used in Chinese medicine for centuries to calm the mind, ease anxiety and support the nervous system. It is considered a grounding, settling material - anchoring restless energy and promoting clarity.

The Name: Succinite

The formal mineralogical name for Baltic amber is succinite, from the Latin succinum, meaning amber. It was Albert the Great - the 13th-century philosopher and naturalist - who recorded the name Succinium, and the term has anchored the material's identity ever since. The same root gives us succinic acid, the compound most closely associated with genuine Baltic amber and the marker used to distinguish it from other fossil resins and imitations.

What It's Made Of

Baltic amber is not a single substance but a complex fossil material built up over 35 to 50 million years. Genuine Baltic amber is uniquely rich in succinic acid - between roughly 3% and 8%, concentrated most heavily in the outer crust of the stone. This succinic acid content is the defining chemical signature of true Baltic amber; other world ambers contain far less or none at all.

Alongside succinic acid, analysis has identified a wide spectrum of naturally occurring compounds - terpenes and sesquiterpenes, diterpenoid resin acids such as abietic and agathic acid, and various volatile aromatics. It is this dense, layered chemistry - laid down by ancient coniferous forests and transformed by deep geological time - that gives the distilled oil its extraordinary depth and its warm, resinous, faintly smoky character.

Why It Is So Rare

Baltic amber is not simply fossilised sap. Only a small fraction of ancient resin ever survives the tens of millions of years required to polymerise into true amber - most degrades or is lost entirely. What remains is gathered slowly: washed from the seabed onto Baltic shores by winter storms, or mined from deep deposits in a narrow region of northern Europe that holds the overwhelming majority of the world's supply.

Turning that stone into an aromatic oil is rarer still. Because the fossil resin has fully hardened, it will not yield to gentle steam. Only dry distillation at very high heat draws the oil out - and the yield is famously low, often under 1% of the material by weight. Between the scarcity of the raw stone and the difficulty of extraction, genuine Baltic amber oil remains one of the least common natural aromatics a perfumer can work with.

A Master Fixative

In perfumery, Baltic amber's most prized quality is its power as a fixative - a base material that slows the evaporation of a fragrance and binds its other notes together, holding the composition on the skin for far longer. Historically, only a handful of natural materials could do this well - ambergris from the sperm whale foremost among them. Baltic amber offers a plant-and-fossil-derived alternative: deep, tenacious and animalic-adjacent, without the cost or the ethical weight of ambergris.

Its scent is unmistakable once known - warm and honeyed, resinous and balsamic, with a dry, smoky, almost leathery undertone. Even in trace amounts it lends a fragrance remarkable staying power and a sense of ancient, sunlit depth. At Profound Naturals it anchors the base of several botanical perfumes, and is offered as a rare single aromatic in its own right.

Curious Properties

Amber has fascinated natural philosophers for millennia. Rubbed against cloth it builds a static charge and attracts light objects - the Greek word for amber, elektron, is the direct root of the word electricity. It is warm to the touch rather than cold like glass or stone, and light enough to float in salt water while sinking in fresh. Soft as gemstones go - around 2 to 2.5 on the Mohs scale - it can be carved, warmed and worked in ways a true mineral never could, which is part of why it has been treasured as both ornament and material since the Stone Age.
