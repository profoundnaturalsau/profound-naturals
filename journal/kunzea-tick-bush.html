Kunzea: the tick bush that earned its keep

Farmers noticed their cattle rubbing against one particular shrub to shake off ticks. That plain observation became one of Tasmania's quietest botanical success stories.

Australian Natives July 2026 5 min read

Some plants arrive with a grand reputation. Kunzea arrived with a nickname: the tick bush. It is not a flattering name, and the plant does not have a flattering look either - a scrubby, mid-sized shrub with small white flowers, easy to walk straight past. But it is one of my favourite Australian natives, and the story of how it got noticed is a good one.

The name comes from an observation, not a laboratory. Farmers watched their cattle deliberately rub up against these bushes, and noticed the animals seemed to suffer less from ticks and biting insects afterwards. Native animals were seen sheltering and sleeping underneath them for the same reason. The plant became "the tick bush" long before anyone distilled a drop of its oil.

A quick myth to clear up

Kunzea sounds like it might be an Aboriginal word. It is not. The genus was named after Gustav Kunze, a German botanist and university director who died in 1851 and, as far as anyone knows, never set foot in the Australian bush his name now labels. The plant does have a deep Indigenous history - Aboriginal communities used the crushed leaves and stems for generations to ease aches, pains, and skin complaints - but the botanical name is pure European taxonomy. It goes by other names too: white kunzea, white cloud, and the less generous poverty bush.

Where the good oil comes from

Kunzea ambigua belongs to the Myrtaceae family - the same broad clan as tea tree, eucalyptus and manuka - and it grows along the cooler coastal strip of south-eastern Australia. But the plants prized for oil come mostly from one corner of the country: north-eastern Tasmania and Flinders Island, where the Tasmanian population produces an oil with a character all its own. The scent is clean, fresh and invigorating - people often say it smells like the Australian bush itself. It is gentler on the skin and far more pleasant to my nose than tea tree, which does similar work but announces itself much more loudly.

The oil is steam distilled from the leaves and fine twigs. Analyses of Tasmanian material typically show alpha-pinene as the dominant constituent, alongside compounds such as spathulenol, globulol and bicyclogermacrene - a profile that gives kunzea its clean, resinous, faintly peppery character and sets it apart from tea tree.

An unprepossessing shrub, and then you smell the oil - the whole clean, resinous breath of the bush in a single bottle.

From bush lore to real research

What lifts kunzea above folklore is that the science has been catching up with the cattle. Research through the University of Tasmania has examined its composition and bioactivity over many years, including work on antimicrobial activity and insect repellency. Kunzea ambigua essential oil has also long been recognised in Australian regulation: it appears in a Therapeutic Goods Administration listing notice dating back to 1999, and a number of finished products made by other Australian companies are listed on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods for specific topical uses. It is still a young ingredient next to tea tree - the research base is growing rather than vast - but the traditional reputation and the modern findings keep pointing the same way.

A note on using it

Kunzea oil is for external use only, and the standard regulatory advice is not to apply it undiluted to skin unless it has been formulated into a cream or carrier, or unless a healthcare professional has advised otherwise. Keep it out of reach of children. Nothing here is medical advice, and I make no claim that my oil treats or prevents any condition.

I include kunzea because it is honest, useful, and unmistakably Australian. It does not pretend to be a rare exotic. It is a hardworking native that earned its place the hard way - by being noticed, one rubbed flank of cattle at a time.
