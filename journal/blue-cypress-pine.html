Blue Cypress: the blue that comes from wood

The oil pours a deep inky blue, which is strange enough on its own. Stranger still is that the colour is not sitting inside the tree waiting to be let out. It is made in the still.

Australian Natives July 2026 6 min read

Cut into a northern cypress pine and you get aromatic heartwood the colour of pale yellow running to streaky gold and brown. Nothing about it suggests blue. Feed that same wood into a still, run it long and slow, and what comes off is one of the few genuinely blue oils in the world, and the only well-known one that comes from timber rather than a flower.

The tree is Callitris intratropica, the northern cypress pine, and it belongs to Australia's tropical north: the Top End of the Northern Territory, across into the Kimberley, and east into far north Queensland. It is a slim, upright conifer with fine blue-grey foliage, deeply furrowed bark, and timber so resistant to termites that dead trees can stand in the landscape for decades after they die. That last detail matters more than it sounds, and I will come back to it.

Botanists have argued about its name for over a century. Some treat it as a species in its own right, others fold it into the widespread Callitris columellaris as a subspecies. The trade never bothered with the debate and settled on Australian blue cypress, which is at least unambiguous about what is in the bottle.

The blue is manufactured, not harvested

The colour comes from azulenes, principally guaiazulene, with chamazulene present as well. Neither compound is in the standing tree. Both form during distillation, out of precursors in the wood that rearrange under heat. The blue is not extracted. It is created.

If that sounds familiar, it is the same trick German chamomile plays. The flower holds a colourless compound called matricin, and it is the still that turns it into the chamazulene responsible for that famous deep blue. Same chemistry, same surprise, entirely different plant. What makes blue cypress unusual is the source material: every other blue oil in common use comes from flowers or leaves. This one comes from heartwood.

200 million
Years of evolutionary history behind the southern cypress lineage this tree belongs to, older than the split between Gondwana and Laurasia. Most southern conifers did not survive Australia's long drying-out. The Callitris line adapted to both drought and fire, and stayed.

What it was used for, long before perfumery

The tree has a documented history of use among Aboriginal groups in the north, in particular the Tiwi people of Bathurst and Melville Islands off the coast from Darwin, who know it as karntirrikani. To the Waguni it is laguni. Recorded uses include washes prepared from the pounded inner bark, applied to sores and to the skin, and use of the tree as an insect repellent. That is a historical record of how the plant was used on Country, not a claim about what a bottle of oil will do for anyone today.

Non-Indigenous interest in the oil is recent by comparison. Commercial distillation only really established itself in the last few decades, which makes blue cypress one of the newer materials in a perfumer's cabinet despite the tree being one of the oldest lineages in it.

A tree that needs the right kind of fire

Here is where the story turns, and it is the part I find hardest to shake.

Northern cypress pine is a fire-sensitive conifer living in one of the most fire-prone landscapes on Earth. Mature trees have thick insulating bark and can shrug off a low, slow fire creeping through leaf litter. Grass fires are a different matter: they burn hotter, with taller flames, and they kill the seedlings and saplings outright. The species has no long-lived seed bank to fall back on, so a run of hot fires does not just injure a stand, it removes the next generation entirely.

The way it survived was by clumping. Groves of these trees shade out grass, and with no grass there is no fuel for a hot fire, so the grove effectively protects its own understorey and lets young trees come through. A landscape burnt in small, cool, scattered patches keeps producing those long-unburnt pockets. A landscape burnt in vast late-season wildfires does not.

For tens of thousands of years, that fine-grained patchwork was the work of Aboriginal fire management. When people were moved off their estates across much of northern Australia in the early twentieth century, the patch mosaic went with them, and the wildfires took over. Because the dead wood resists termites and stands for years, the collapse is visible from the air: skeletons where the groves were.

On a continuously occupied Arnhem Land estate, researchers counted around 27 living trees per hectare. In comparable country inside Kakadu, depopulated a century ago, the figure was closer to one, with no seedlings or saplings found at all.

Both areas once carried similar populations. The difference is who was managing the fire. Later attempts to fix it with broad-scale early-season burning have not brought the trees back, which is the uncomfortable finding: some of this may be past the point where changing the fire regime is enough.

Worth being clear about what that does and does not mean for a bottle of oil. Blue cypress in the trade is generally distilled from cultivated and managed stands rather than cut from the remnant wild groves that are in trouble. The decline is a land management story, not a fragrance industry one. But it is the reason I will not describe this tree as simply abundant, and it is a reminder that a plant can be common in a catalogue and quietly disappearing in the country it came from.

What it actually smells like

Very little like cypress, if what you have in mind is the crisp green needle oil from the Mediterranean Cupressus sempervirens. Blue cypress is a wood oil and behaves like one: smoky, resinous, faintly sweet, with a honeyed warmth underneath and a dry balsamic finish. It sits closer to sandalwood and guaiacwood than to anything in the cypress needle family. I stock both, and putting them side by side is the fastest way to understand that a shared common name can mean almost nothing.

In blending it earns its place twice over. It has real character of its own, and it is tenacious enough to act as a fixative, holding lighter material in place and giving a composition a dark floor to stand on. It is the smoke and ancient wood in the middle of Jazz Lounge, and it sits under the amber and sandalwood in Velvet Forest.

What keeps me coming back to it is the same thing that makes it difficult. It is a tree that only exists in numbers where people are actively looking after the country it grows in, and an oil whose most striking quality does not exist until the moment it is made. Very little about it is straightforward, and none of it is accidental.
